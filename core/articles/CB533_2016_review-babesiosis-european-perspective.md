## Abstract-derived summary

This narrative review gathers published knowledge on canine babesiosis in Europe, a tick-borne disease caused by several protozoan Babesia species. Both large forms (B. canis, B. vogeli) and small forms (B. gibsoni and the B. microti-like isolates, also called B. vulpes or Theileria annae) infect European dogs, and the species differ in geographic range, route of transmission, clinical presentation, response to treatment and outlook. The authors present the material as question-and-answer guidance for veterinary practitioners, covering diagnosis, treatment and prevention. The stated aim is to answer the questions most often raised in practice, drawing on the growing body of European data collected over many years.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** review
- **Authors' conclusion:** Information on canine babesiosis in Europe has grown considerably. Canine babesiosis is a group of diseases caused by several species, so accurate detection and species identification are needed to choose treatment and judge prognosis (full text).
- **Reviewer interpretation:** For the pathophysiology question, the most relevant part is the section and table on clinical signs and laboratory abnormalities, which compares severity, laboratory findings and prognosis across the four European species. The review also lists complications (renal, coagulation, hepatic, acid-base, respiratory) and reports poor outcome in B. canis infection as associated with several laboratory variables (full text). These are summaries of other studies, so they show associations at best.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** narrative review with no search strategy or appraisal method described in the text read; written as a practitioner guideline, so most content covers epidemiology, diagnosis, treatment and prevention; evidence for the small Babesia species is described as scarce and often from single case reports (full text); no new host-side data.

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - The review is organised as a set of practitioner questions; one section covers clinical signs and laboratory abnormalities, with a table comparing B. canis, B. vogeli, B. gibsoni and B. microti-like sp. for history, severity, laboratory findings, course and prognosis (full text)
  - Severity is described as mild to moderate for B. vogeli and moderate to severe for the other three species; prognosis is good to poor for B. canis, good for B. vogeli and guarded to poor for B. gibsoni and B. microti-like sp. (full text)
  - Anaemia is attributed by the authors to a combination of intravascular and extravascular haemolysis, increased osmotic fragility of red cells and secondary immune-mediated processes; non-regenerative anaemia is described as more typical of B. canis (full text)
  - Listed complications include nephropathy and glomerulonephritis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, immune-mediated haemolysis or thrombocytopenia, shock, acid-base disturbance, pancreatitis, rhabdomyolysis and acute respiratory distress, noted as especially frequent after B. rossi infection (full text)
  - Disease severity is reported to be associated with parasite density in B. rossi infection, while parasite density did not differ between survivors and non-survivors in one study of B. canis (full text)
  - In B. canis infection, poor outcome and mortality are reported to be associated with moderate anaemia, severe thrombocytopenia, hyperlactataemia and other laboratory changes; one cited study reported 8 of 15 dogs (53%) dying within 24-48 h of presentation (full text)
  - Azotemia is reported as the main cause of death in B. microti-like sp. infection, with a mortality rate of 22%, although pre-renal azotemia was not fully excluded (full text)
  - Reported mortality of 28-70% is cited when prednisone is recommended for dogs with moderate-to-severe immune-mediated haemolytic anaemia or thrombocytopenia (full text)
