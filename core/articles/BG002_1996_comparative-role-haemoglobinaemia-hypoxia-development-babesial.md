## Abstract-derived summary

The authors asked whether free haemoglobin in plasma or oxygen shortage from anaemia is the main cause of the kidney injury seen in canine babesiosis. Eighteen dogs formed three groups of six and were followed for four days. One group received an infusion of dog haemoglobin, one was made anaemic to cause hypoxia, and one received both. Urine and blood were tested daily; at the end, filtration rate was measured and kidneys examined microscopically. Hypoxia alone gave more urinary casts, more shed tubular cells, higher urinary enzyme activity and a lower filtration rate. Haemoglobin infusion did not raise urinary enzymes. Tissue changes were mild single-cell tubular death in most dogs. The authors concluded that severe haemoglobinaemia caused no significant nephropathy, hypoxia a very mild one, and that the lesions differed from natural disease.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** experimental animal study (controlled, small groups); mechanistic, not a clinical-outcome study.
- **Relevance:** Cited in Q1 (haemolysis, free haem, endothelial activation and tubular injury) as the stated counter-hypothesis: experimental haemoglobinaemia alone did not cause nephropathy in dogs, so any association between plasma cell-free haemoglobin and tubular injury in infected dogs may depend on co-factors such as hypoxia rather than a direct haemoglobin effect. It is also the main reason the novelty check for Q1 treats a simple "free haemoglobin causes kidney injury" model as unlikely.
- **Key limitations:** Only 6 dogs per group over 4 days, so short exposure and low power; the abstract notes large individual variation in handling infused haemoglobin. Infused homologous haemoglobin in uninfected dogs does not reproduce the inflammation, endothelial activation, acidosis or parasite-related factors of natural infection; the authors themselves state that the lesions differed from those of canine babesiosis. Whether haem, as opposed to haemoglobin, was assessed is not stated in the abstract.
