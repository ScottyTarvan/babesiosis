## Abstract-derived summary

Pigmented serum from free haemoglobin or bilirubin is common in dogs with babesiosis and disturbs photometric assays, including urea and creatinine, which complicates the diagnosis of renal failure. Severely anaemic dogs show a disproportionately high urea of unknown origin, giving a raised urea-to-creatinine ratio. The authors used cystatin-C, a marker of glomerular filtration unaffected by haemoglobin, as a reference. They compared severely anaemic infected dogs with a babesiosis-free reference group. Urea and the urea:creatinine ratio were significantly higher in the infected dogs, while creatinine and cystatin-C stayed inside reference ranges. The authors concluded that the raised ratio reflects increased urea that is probably not renal in origin, that creatinine was as specific as cystatin-C for renal function here, that creatinine sensitivity was not established, and that urea is not a suitable marker for diagnosing renal failure in these dogs.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** associational
- **Authors' conclusion:** The raised urea:creatinine ratio in severely anaemic canine babesiosis reflects raised urea with normal creatinine and cystatin-C, and is most likely of non-renal origin; urea should not be used to diagnose renal failure in this disease.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** In this group, urea elevation was not accompanied by changes in the two filtration markers, so it was not associated with reduced glomerular filtration. The study does not identify what raises urea; the authors list laboratory interference, pre-renal azotaemia and increased urea production as candidates. It supports interpreting creatinine rather than urea when screening for renal injury in babesiosis.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** small single-centre sample (25 infected, 13 controls; full text); only severely anaemic dogs were enrolled, so the findings may not apply to other forms of the disease; species not identified; sensitivity of creatinine for renal dysfunction not established; no direct measure of glomerular filtration; cause of the raised urea not determined; photometric interference by free haemoglobin not excluded; a human cystatin-C assay was used (full text).

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - Prospective study at the Onderstepoort Veterinary Academic Hospital with 25 dogs with babesiosis and 13 clinically healthy reference dogs (full text)
  - Inclusion required parasites on a thin blood smear, no macroscopic serum lipaemia and a low packed cell volume (0.2 or lower; unit garbled in the converted text); controls were smear negative (full text)
  - Samples were taken before any treatment; groups were compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (full text)
  - Mean urea was 12.8 in infected dogs against 5.1 in controls (P < 0.001; units printed as "mmol/" in the converted text, denominator lost); urea was above the reference range in 17 of 25 infected dogs (full text)
  - Mean urea:creatinine ratio was 46.3 in infected dogs against 15.9 in controls (P < 0.001), and was raised in 23 of 25 infected and 3 of 13 control dogs (full text)
  - Mean creatinine was 72.1 against 78.7 (P = 0.27) and mean cystatin-C 0.71 against 0.67 (P = 0.45), with units printed as "µmol/" and "mg/" in the converted text; neither was raised in any infected dog (full text)
  - Mean free serum haemoglobin was 1.72 in infected dogs against 0.88 in controls (P = 0.002; printed as "g/" in the converted text); it exceeded 1.6 in 8 of 25 infected dogs (full text)
  - Three haemoconcentrated cases seen during the collection period were described separately: two recovered, and one with acute renal failure confirmed at post mortem had urea 41.8, creatinine 608, cystatin-C 5.7 (units as above) and a ratio of 17.02 (full text)
  - The authors advise measuring creatinine and hourly urine production, and saw no added benefit from cystatin-C (full text)
