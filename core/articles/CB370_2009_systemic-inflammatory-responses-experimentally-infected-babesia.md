## Abstract-derived summary

Researchers followed dogs experimentally given low, moderate or high doses of Babesia canis-parasitised red blood cells, aiming to clarify early pathogenesis through detailed haematology. Plasma C-reactive protein rose before parasites appeared in the blood, pointing to an acute-phase reaction. Other findings included fever, raised fibrinogen, falling platelet and white cell counts, and a longer coagulation time that accompanied the low platelet counts. Dogs also became dangerously hypotensive, and those given the largest inoculum needed anti-parasitic treatment. Lower blood pressure went together with a lower packed cell volume, which in turn tracked lower plasma creatinine, a pattern the authors read as expanded plasma volume diluting both. The dose influenced when the changes began, not their time course, so curves were displaced in time but similar in shape, which the authors interpret as a fixed inflammatory programme set off by infection.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** mechanistic (experimental)
- **Authors' conclusion:** The onset, but not the dynamics, of the response depended on the infectious dose, indicating that infection triggered a preset inflammatory response.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The study describes the early time course of acute-phase, coagulation and haemodynamic changes in experimentally infected dogs, with the CRP rise preceding detectable parasitaemia. The link between hypotension, lower packed cell volume and lower creatinine is an association, and the plasma volume expansion is the authors' suggestion rather than a measured result in the abstract. The dose-shifted but similar curves support a stereotyped host response, but the abstract gives no statistics.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** group sizes and statistics not reported in the abstract; species identified only as B. canis with no molecular method stated; experimental inoculation may not match natural infection; abstract-only reading.
