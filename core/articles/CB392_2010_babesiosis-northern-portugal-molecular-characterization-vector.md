## Abstract-derived summary

The authors examined 45 dogs from northern Portugal that were clinically suspected of babesiosis, using blood smears, PCR and DNA sequencing to identify Babesia and co-infecting Leishmania, Anaplasma/Ehrlichia and Hepatozoon. Nearly all animals (44, or 98%) carried Babesia canis canis, and one carried B. canis vogeli. Nine dogs (20%) had additional pathogens, most often Leishmania infantum, followed by Ehrlichia canis and Hepatozoon canis, the latter being the first molecular detection of that parasite in dogs of the region. Almost 90% of cases were diagnosed in the colder months, October to March. Half of the 44 B. canis-infected dogs carried ticks. Four dogs (9%) died or were euthanized: two co-infected animals died on their own, and two singly infected animals were put down. PCR detected Babesia more often than microscopy.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** descriptive
- **Authors' conclusion:** Babesia canis canis is the main agent of canine babesiosis in northern Portugal, PCR was more sensitive than blood smears, and about one fifth of the dogs were co-infected with L. infantum, E. canis or H. canis.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** This is a descriptive case series with a mainly epidemiological aim. Its host-side content is limited to clinical signs, haematocrit and outcome. Co-infected dogs had a higher crude number of deaths (2 of 9) than singly infected dogs (none died spontaneously), but the groups are very small, no test of mortality is reported, and the deaths in the singly infected group were euthanasia at the owners' request. The data do not show that co-infection worsens outcome.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** no uninfected control group; dogs clinically suspected of babesiosis only; no complete blood count, serum biochemistry or urinalysis (full text); haematocrit missing for 12 dogs and clinical signs reported for only 38 of 45 (full text); only nine co-infected dogs; deaths and euthanasia combined in the outcome; only one dog with B. canis vogeli.

_Abstract-level appraisal; not a formal risk-of-bias assessment._

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - Dogs came from Alto Trás-os-Montes and Douro and were sampled between October 2007 and March 2009; none had a travel history to southern Portugal or Spain (full text)
  - The 45 dogs were 24 males and 21 females; age (known for 38) ranged from 2 months to 14 years with a median of 3.0 years; there were 16 mongrels and Podengo was the most common breed (n = 17) (full text)
  - Clinical signs in 38 examined dogs included lethargy (24; 63%), red urine (19; 50%), hyperthermia (18; 47%), anorexia (17; 45%) and pale mucous membranes (17; 45%) (full text)
  - 26 of 33 dogs (79%) were anaemic, with haematocrit below the reference interval of 37-55%; haematocrit was not measured in 12 dogs (full text)
  - Blood smears showed large intraerythrocytic piroplasms in 41 dogs (91%); all 45 dogs were PCR-positive for Babesia (full text)
  - Haematocrit did not differ significantly between eight co-infected dogs and 25 singly infected dogs (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.449); age also did not differ (full text)
  - 41 dogs recovered after imidocarb dipropionate treatment (seven co-infected and 34 singly infected); the two euthanased dogs were infected with B. canis canis only (full text)
  - The dog that died with B. canis vogeli, E. canis and L. infantum had a haematocrit of 3% (full text, Table 3)
  - Table 3 gives sex, breed, age, clinical signs, haematocrit, tick status, month and outcome for each of the nine co-infected dogs; seven recovered and two died (full text, Table 3)
  - The authors state that a complete clinicopathological evaluation, in particular blood cell counts, was not performed (full text)
