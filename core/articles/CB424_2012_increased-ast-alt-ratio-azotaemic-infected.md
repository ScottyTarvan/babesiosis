## Abstract-derived summary

Researchers measured the AST/ALT ratio in 182 Babesia canis-infected dogs, of whom 65 were anaemic and 68 were azotaemic. The study compared the ratio between anaemic and non-anaemic dogs, and between azotaemic and non-azotaemic dogs. Mean ratios were similar in anaemic and non-anaemic dogs (1.52 vs 1.76, p = 0.23). Azotaemic dogs had a significantly higher mean ratio than non-azotaemic dogs (2.68 vs 1.08, p = 0.00). Group means were compared with Student's t test using p < 0.05 as the significance threshold. Overall, the authors suggest that kidney injury may have contributed to the raised AST activity in these dogs, as the anaemia comparison was not significant.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** associational
- **Authors' conclusion:** Kidney injury appears to have contributed to the raised AST activity in infected dogs (abstract); the full text adds that the ratio may be a useful aid in diagnosing kidney injury, pending reference intervals (full text).
- **Reviewer interpretation:** A higher AST/ALT ratio was associated with azotaemia but not with anaemia in B. canis-infected dogs. Because the design is a cross-sectional comparison without histology or a renal-specific marker, the link to kidney injury is inferred, not demonstrated. Azotaemia may also have non-renal causes, and the AST source was not identified.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** no healthy control group; species not confirmed by molecular methods; probably a single clinic setting (inferred from the affiliations; full text); no reference interval or cut-off for the ratio (full text); no direct renal injury marker or histology; samples taken from the 230 dogs of a previous study, so the dogs overlap with that paper (full text).

_Abstract-level appraisal; not a formal risk-of-bias assessment._

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - Samples came from a previous study in which whole blood and serum were collected before treatment from 230 B. canis-infected dogs; 48 samples were excluded because azotaemia could not be clearly identified (full text)
  - Group sizes were 22 anaemic and azotaemic, 43 anaemic and non-azotaemic, 46 non-anaemic and azotaemic, and 71 non-anaemic and non-azotaemic (full text)
  - Anaemia was found in 35.7% (65/182) and azotaemia in 37.4% (68/182) of samples (full text)
  - AST was above the reference interval in 169 of 182 samples (92.9%) and ALT in 119 of 182 (65.4%); the mean AST/ALT ratio overall was 1.68 ± 1.28 (full text)
  - The AST/ALT ratio correlated with serum urea (r = 0.68) and creatinine (r = 0.62) (full text)
  - Authors are affiliated with Warsaw University of Life Sciences, the Multiwet small animal clinic (Warsaw) and the W. Stefański Institute of Parasitology; no data availability or ethics statement was seen in the text (full text)
  - The text names the parasite as B. canis and states no diagnostic method (full text)
  - The authors suggest the AST/ALT ratio may be a useful diagnostic aid for kidney injury, but reference intervals and a cut-off value still need to be determined (full text)
  - The authors note that urea and creatinine can rise in canine babesiosis through non-renal factors, citing De Scally et al. (2004) (full text)
