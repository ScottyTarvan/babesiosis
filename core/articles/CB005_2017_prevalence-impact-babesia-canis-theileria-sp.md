## Abstract-derived summary

Piroplasm infection in free-ranging canids was poorly characterised, so the authors combined post-mortem work with molecular testing in Croatian wolves. Carcasses of 107 wild wolves and one zoo-kept animal were examined, and blood from one live-captured wolf was also tested. 18S rRNA PCR followed by sequencing detected piroplasm DNA in 21 of 108 wild wolves plus the captive animal; seven of the 22 positives carried Babesia canis and 15 had sequences matching Theileria capreoli, a parasite of deer. Parasitaemia was very low and cytology showed ring and pear-shaped forms. The captive wolf died, with the authors attributing this to sepsis following haemolytic anaemia, whereas haemolytic disease was largely absent in the wild wolves. They propose wolves may be natural hosts and dogs secondary hosts.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** descriptive
- **Authors' conclusion:** B. canis causes little disease in wolves, which may indicate wolves are the natural host and dogs a secondary host.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The captive 17-year-old wolf's necropsy (see below) resembles severe complicated canine babesiosis, a useful comparative phenotype. The tolerance claim is hypothesis only: most wild animals were trauma deaths with autolysis, low parasite loads and sparse haematology, so subclinical carriage versus survivorship bias cannot be separated. One severe case cannot establish a species-level mechanism.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** single severe case; age, captivity and possible comorbidity confound; histopathology on only 26 non-autolytic carcasses (full text); haematology on two wolves (full text); no controls; capture stress may explain raised CK in the live wolf.

_Abstract-level appraisal; not a formal risk-of-bias assessment._
