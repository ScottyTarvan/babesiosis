## Abstract-derived summary

This narrative review outlines canine babesiosis, a tick-borne disease that also affects humans and many other domestic and wild animals. Among domestic animals, dogs, cats, cattle and horses show appreciable illness and death. Canine and feline babesiosis share a core picture of haemolytic anaemia, jaundice and dark urine. The course in dogs can be subclinical or chronic, or peracute and fatal, depending on the virulence of the parasite and the susceptibility of the host. The paper covers pathogenesis, clinical findings, complications, diagnosis and treatment of the disease. It also reports newly identified prognostic factors for outcome. The focus is canine babesiosis, with specific reference to Babesia rossi infection.

  - The three large canine Babesia named are B. vogeli (least pathogenic), B. canis (intermediate) and B. rossi (most virulent); the small forms named are B. gibsoni and B. annae (Theileria annae) (full text)
  - Mortality is given as about 12% for B. rossi and about 1% for B. vogeli, and overall survival as roughly 85-90% of cases (full text)
  - Signs are attributed in the review to tissue hypoxia after anaemia and a systemic inflammatory response with cytokine release; the anaemia is said to be unrelated to parasitaemia and its pathogenesis only partly understood, with intravascular and extravascular haemolysis and a possible role for poor marrow response (full text)
  - Severe disease is described with acid-base disturbance and complications including acute renal failure, hepatopathy, hypoglycaemia, ARDS, cerebral disease and immune-mediated haemolysis (full text)
  - A small group of dogs with a high haematocrit (haemoconcentration) is described as being at higher risk of renal or cerebral complications (full text)
  - Pancreatitis is reported with a 20% mortality rate and frequent co-occurring complications (full text)
  - Thrombocytopenia is described as a hallmark whose pathophysiology is undetermined (full text)
  - Prognostic findings listed include admission lactate (more than 5 mmol/L associated with higher mortality), hypoglycaemia (glucose below 3.3 mmol/L in about 20% of cases), higher median admission cortisol in dogs that died (482 versus 150 nmol/L), lower thyroxine in non-survivors, higher cardiac troponin I in a 3-dog non-survivor group versus 31 others, and a positive association between high parasitaemia and mortality (full text)
  - Outcome is said to depend on the organ system affected (lungs, kidneys, brain) rather than on SIRS status or the number of organs involved (full text)
  - Primary studies cited in the text include Keller et al. 2004, Schoeman et al. 2007, Böhm et al. 2006 and Welzl et al. 2001 (full text)
