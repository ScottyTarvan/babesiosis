## Abstract-derived summary

This narrative review asks why canine babesiosis in Southern Africa is so severe, with pups and juveniles badly affected. The author contrasts this with bovine babesiosis, where host, parasite and vector are thought to have co-evolved, so young animals become immune without showing signs. Babesia rossi, the main agent in sub-Saharan Africa, was originally identified in a side-striped jackal, and sparse data suggest indigenous African canids can carry it without apparent harm. Because dogs are not native to Africa, most dogs seen at South African clinics come from recently introduced breeds that have had little time to adapt. The author suggests that intensive treatment of clinical cases effectively negates natural selection, so the status quo will probably persist. The author also proposes that B. vogeli, which often causes mild or unapparent infection, is closest to the ancestral canine parasite, possibly from wolves.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** review
- **Authors' conclusion:** The author contends that B. rossi is a novel challenge that recently imported dogs have not yet adapted to, and that intensive treatment of cases will probably keep the status quo in place indefinitely.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The article offers a hypothesis about the evolutionary origin of high virulence, not host-side pathophysiological measurements. Its link to the review question is the contrast in severity between B. rossi, B. canis and B. vogeli in dogs. The argument rests on comparison with bovine and wildlife babesiosis and on sparse data from African canids, so it is associational and speculative.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** narrative review with no stated search method (full text); evidence on African canids is described by the author as meagre; the hypothesis is not tested; older literature may not identify the causative species (full text); no new dog-level data.

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - Babesiosis is diagnosed in about 10% of dogs presented to South African veterinary practices; at the Onderstepoort Veterinary Academic Hospital about 12% of sick dogs are diagnosed with it, and about 31% of these are admitted for intensive treatment (full text)
  - Clinical signs of B. rossi infection are attributed to tissue hypoxia from anaemia and a systemic inflammatory response with marked cytokine release; severe disease involves haemolytic anaemia, acid-base derangements and complications such as acute renal failure, hepatopathy, hypoglycaemia, ARDS, cerebral pathology and immune-mediated erythrocyte destruction; mortality is stated as around 12% (full text)
  - B. vogeli infection in adult dogs is often unapparent or moderate with low parasitaemia, although pups can present with marked anaemia; B. canis is described as intermediate in pathogenicity (full text)
  - Each large canine Babesia taxon is tied to a different tick vector: B. rossi to Haemaphysalis elliptica, B. canis to Dermacentor reticulatus, B. vogeli to Rhipicephalus sanguineus (full text)
  - Pre-1990 literature may not identify the causative species; the author attributes most South African pathogenesis literature to B. rossi (full text)
  - In a large breeding centre, 17 of 227 (7.0%) wild dog blood specimens were B. rossi positive with no clinical babesiosis observed; black-backed jackals infected experimentally by blood subinoculation developed parasitaemia without overt signs (full text)
  - Whether the indigenous Africanis dog breed has become resistant or tolerant to B. rossi is stated to be unknown (full text)
