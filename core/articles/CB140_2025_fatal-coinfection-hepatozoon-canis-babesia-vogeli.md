## Abstract-derived summary

The authors describe a dog with heavy ectoparasite infestation that presented in clinical shock and died. Blood smears and a full necropsy were done, and organ samples went for histology and molecular testing. Smears showed azurophilic inclusions resembling Ehrlichia canis, gametocytes in leukocytes resembling Hepatozoon canis, and pear-shaped intra-erythrocytic forms consistent with large piroplasms. Acridine orange staining of tissue sections revealed fluorescent structures morphologically resembling Bartonella. Molecular assays were positive for Ehrlichia, Hepatozoon, Babesia and Bartonella. The authors argue that vector-borne disease control would benefit animal and human health in resource-limited tropical regions, and that such infections belong in the differential diagnosis wherever ticks and fleas occur. Lesion descriptions are not given in the abstract.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** descriptive (case report)
- **Authors' conclusion:** Vector-borne diseases should always be suspected in areas with ticks and fleas, and better control would improve animal and human health.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** Illustrates multi-pathogen vector-borne co-infection ending in shock and death, but the Babesia contribution cannot be isolated from E. canis, H. canis and Bartonella. It offers no mechanistic data in the abstract; its value is as a reminder of co-infection confounding in severe or fatal field cases. Low weight for pathophysiology.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** n=1; four pathogens; Babesia species assignment method not stated in abstract (title names B. vogeli); Bartonella supported partly by morphology; no antemortem haematology or biochemistry or histological lesions in abstract; full text not accessible.
