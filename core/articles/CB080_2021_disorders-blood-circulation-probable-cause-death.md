## Abstract-derived summary

The study set out to characterise post-mortem lesions in dogs that died of B. canis infection and to infer the most likely cause of death. Six cadavers underwent necropsy, and tissues from several organs were examined histologically. Congestion and oedema were found across organs. Most dogs had ascites, hydrothorax or hydropericardium, together with pulmonary oedema, congestion of lung, kidney, liver and brain, and cardiomyocyte necrosis. The authors concluded that disordered blood circulation was the most probable cause of death, while noting that lung inflammation and cerebral babesiosis in some dogs might also have contributed. They further suggested renal congestion may promote renal hypoxia and azotaemia.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** descriptive
- **Authors' conclusion:** Disordered blood circulation was the most probable cause of death, with pulmonary inflammation and cerebral babesiosis as possible alternatives.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** Useful morphological evidence of multi-organ congestion, effusions and myocardial injury consistent with a shock-like, circulatory phenotype in fatal B. canis infection. Cause of death is inferred, not demonstrated, and the pathogenesis (vascular leak, myocardial dysfunction, hypoxia, or treatment effects) is not resolved. The renal congestion-hypoxia hypothesis is speculative.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** Very small, self-selected sample (6 of 17 deaths, owner consent). No survivors or non-babesia controls. All dogs died within 1-2 days of admission and received intravenous fluids and other interventions, which may contribute to oedema and congestion. Post-mortem autolysis and pre-existing chronic lesions could confound findings. One dog had Toxocara co-infection.

_Abstract-level appraisal; not a formal risk-of-bias assessment._
