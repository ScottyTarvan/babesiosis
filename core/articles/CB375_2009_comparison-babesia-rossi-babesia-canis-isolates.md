## Abstract-derived summary

The two large Babesia species, B. canis and B. rossi, are responsible for clinical disease in dogs with a wide range of presentations. This narrative review brings together field and experimental observations suggesting that B. rossi is the more virulent of the two, a difference that appeared linked to parasitaemia. It is unclear whether this reflects a species-level difference or variation in virulence between individual isolates. Laboratory and animal comparisons revealed several qualitative differences between isolates, but their causal relevance is unresolved. A bivalent vaccine made from soluble parasite antigens of in vitro culture supernatants protected dogs from clinical babesiosis after challenge with either species. In vaccinated dogs, parasitaemia was effectively controlled after B. rossi challenge, whereas B. canis challenge had little or no effect on parasitaemia, although plasma antigen levels fell.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** review
- **Authors' conclusion:** Field and experimental data suggest that B. rossi is more virulent than B. canis, although isolate variability may explain some of this. A bivalent soluble-antigen vaccine protected against clinical disease with either species, and the nature of vaccine-induced immunity appears to differ with the challenge species.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The review is most relevant to virulence, parasitaemia dynamics and immune responses in B. rossi compared with B. canis infection, with inflammation (fibrinogen) and sequestration offered only as hypotheses. The comparisons rest on a small number of isolates, mostly studied by the authors' own group (full text), so the species-level contrast cannot be separated from isolate-level variation.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** narrative review with no stated search method; two isolates per species; most experimental evidence from one research group with vaccine-industry affiliation; virulence differences confounded by isolate variability; authors state that causal links between in vitro and in vivo characteristics are unproven; some data cited as personal communication or in preparation; little direct host pathophysiology beyond fibrinogen and sequestration hypotheses.

_Abstract-level appraisal; not a formal risk-of-bias assessment._

  - B. canis-infected red cells could be partially purified over Plasmagel and tended to autoagglutinate in vitro (cited as a personal communication), whereas B. rossi-infected red cells did not (full text)
  - Unfixed-cell immunofluorescence indicated species-specific surface epitopes; in the adapted table, anti-dog immunoglobulin labelled 20-30% of B. rossi-infected and 0% of B. canis-infected cells (full text)
  - In age-matched experimental infections, B. rossi-infected dogs developed progressive parasitaemia needing treatment at day 7, whereas B. canis-infected dogs showed limited transient parasitaemia; timing of parasitaemia control did not correlate with clinical recovery in B. canis infection (full text)
  - Fibrinogen increase was reported as higher in B. canis than in B. rossi infection, and coating of infected red cells with fibrinogen is proposed, but not proven, to affect autoagglutination and sequestration (full text)
  - Vaccination with B. rossi soluble antigen alone was not protective, and repeated doses did not improve efficacy; with the bivalent vaccine after three vaccinations no infected red cells were seen in smears after B. rossi challenge (full text)
