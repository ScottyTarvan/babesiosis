## Abstract-derived summary

The authors tested whether in situ hybridization with a digoxigenin-labelled DNA probe could reveal Babesia gibsoni in blood smears and in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues from infected dogs. In smears fixed with paraformaldehyde, parasites inside and outside erythrocytes from culture were specifically labelled, showing that parasite genomic DNA could be visualised this way. In tissue sections, the probe also labelled the parasite in spleen, lymph node and kidney. Staining was seen in infected red cells within splenic and renal blood vessels, in macrophages laden with haemosiderin in the spleen, and in engulfed red cells in lymph nodes that appeared parasitised. The authors propose that the approach could help study the parasite's life cycle and the pathology of canine babesiosis.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** descriptive (method development with qualitative histology)
- **Authors' conclusion:** In situ hybridization detected B. gibsoni in blood smears and in fixed tissues, and the authors suggest it can be used to study the parasite's life cycle and the pathological condition of canine babesiosis.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The main contribution is a detection protocol; its host-side content is a qualitative description of parasite DNA signal in infected erythrocytes in splenic and renal vessels and in macrophages of spleen and lymph nodes. This is consistent with erythrophagocytosis and with infected cells being present in organ vessels. Because the probe targets genomic DNA, signal inside macrophages cannot show whether the parasites were alive. Endothelial adhesion of infected erythrocytes is raised by the authors as a hypothesis only.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** two experimentally infected, splenectomised beagles (full text; the figure legends refer to a single dog); qualitative findings with no counts or statistics; no uninfected-dog tissue described as a hybridization control (full text; controls were a canine 18S probe and probe blocking); liver labelling was judged unsuccessful (full text); the probe shares 80-85% sequence identity with Hsp70 of other Babesia species, including three B. canis subspecies that these dogs were not infected with (full text); the smears used for hybridization were prepared from in vitro culture, not from dog blood, although the abstract describes the smears as from infected dogs (full text); the adhesion hypothesis was not tested.

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - Two adult female beagles were inoculated with 1 x 10^9 parasites from an experimentally, chronically infected dog, were splenectomised on day 11 after inoculation, and were killed at the onset of clinically apparent lethargy (full text)
  - The parasite strain came from a naturally infected dog in Nagasaki, Japan, in 1973, and had been kept in dogs and, recently, in in vitro culture (full text)
  - The smears examined were prepared from the in vitro culture, not from the blood of the infected dogs; smears fixed with methanol gave no probe signal, while paraformaldehyde-fixed smears did (full text)
  - The probe was a 516 bp fragment of the B. gibsoni Hsp70 gene; its sequence identity with Hsp70 of B. canis rossi, B. canis vogeli and B. canis canis was 85.0, 84.4 and 84.1% by the authors' comparison, so cross-hybridization with those species was thought possible, but the dogs were not infected with them (full text)
  - Tissues examined were spleen, lymph node, kidney and liver from the experimentally infected dogs (full text)
  - H&E sections of splenic red pulp showed haemosiderosis and small numbers of infected erythrocytes, and the same areas were labelled by the probe; infected erythrocytes in splenic and renal vessels were hard to see on H&E (full text)
  - In lymph nodes, many erythrocytes engulfed by macrophages appeared parasitised and were labelled by the probe (full text)
  - The authors suggest that parasite DNA may persist in macrophages after the parasites die, but the duration was not determined (full text)
  - The authors hypothesise that infected erythrocytes may adhere to vascular endothelium in the spleen and kidney, and state that further work is needed to confirm this (full text)
  - In liver, only macrophage-like cells were rarely labelled, and the protocol was judged unsuccessful for this tissue; brain and bone marrow were not examined (full text)
  - The study was approved by the Laboratory Animal committee of the Graduate School of Veterinary Medicine, Hokkaido University (approval number 8099) (full text)
