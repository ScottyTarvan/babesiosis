## Abstract-derived summary

Researchers tested bruceine A, a quassinoid compound obtained from the dried fruit of Brucea javanica, against Babesia gibsoni. In canine red cell cultures the compound suppressed parasite growth at lower concentrations than diminazene aceturate, and parasites were destroyed within a day at 25 nM. One dog given the compound orally for five days showed no clinical abnormalities, and its blood values stayed in the normal range. Three dogs were then infected with B. gibsoni; two received the compound daily for six days beginning on day five after infection. The untreated dog developed marked anaemia, high fever and total loss of appetite and activity. The two treated dogs stayed clinically well over four weeks, although parasites were not cleared from the blood and packed cell volume, red cell and platelet counts fell. The authors propose the compound as a candidate treatment.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** descriptive (small experimental infection and treatment trial)
- **Authors' conclusion:** Bruceine A or other related compounds are potential candidates for the treatment of canine babesiosis (abstract). The full text also calls bruceine A a potent antibabesial compound (full text).
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The in vivo arm is a three-dog experimental infection in which the host measurements (PCV, platelets, temperature) serve as efficacy endpoints. It describes severe anaemia in the untreated dog and falls in platelet counts after B. gibsoni inoculation, including in the two treated dogs that stayed clinically well. Host mechanisms were not examined, so the study adds descriptive haematological data rather than pathophysiological explanation.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** only three infected dogs and one untreated control; no replication, and no statistical analysis of the in vivo data (full text); several results reported as data not shown (full text); untreated dog reached the humane endpoint on day 14, so its later course is unknown (full text); treatment efficacy was the aim and host pathophysiology was not investigated.

_Abstract-level appraisal; not a formal risk-of-bias assessment._

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - Four 10-month-old male beagles were used. Dog A was uninfected and received bruceine A to check side-effects (full text).
  - Dogs B, C and D were inoculated intravenously with 1.2 x 10^9 B. gibsoni-parasitised erythrocytes taken from a chronically infected dog. Dogs B and C received 6.4 mg/kg/day orally for 6 days from day 5; dog D received glucose powder in a capsule (full text).
  - The B. gibsoni strain was isolated from a naturally infected dog in Nagasaki in 1973 and has been maintained in dogs at Hokkaido University (full text).
  - Untreated dog D had a peak parasitaemia of 2.3% on day 13, pallor from day 12 and a temperature of 40.4 C on day 14, and reached the humane endpoint on day 14 with a PCV of 15% (full text).
  - In treated dog C the PCV fell to a nadir of 18% on day 15 and then recovered to about 30 to 40%; in treated dog B the PCV stayed above 28% (full text).
  - Platelet counts were about 300,000/uL before infection and fell below 10,000/uL on day 14 in all three infected dogs. Dog B stayed below 55,000/uL; dog C rose to 240,000/uL on day 21 and then fell again (full text).
  - Serum biochemistry and electrolytes were within reference ranges apart from one ALT value of 136 U/L in dog B on day 4 (reference 17 to 78 U/L) (full text).
  - Real-time PCR copy number of the p18 gene reached a first peak on day 11 at 4.3, 8.0 and 7.0 copies per 100 erythrocytes in dogs B, C and D, and did not parallel microscopic parasitaemia (full text).
  - The authors note that thrombocytopenia was more marked than anaemia, as reported in earlier B. gibsoni studies (full text).
  - No data availability statement, supplement or repository accession is given in the text (full text).
