## Abstract-derived summary

This study examined serum and urine electrolyte indices in hospitalised dogs with babesiosis caused by Babesia canis. The main measures were the SUSPPUP ratio and the fractional excretion of strong monovalent electrolytes. According to the abstract, both the SUSPPUP ratio and the fractional excretion of potassium were raised in infected dogs, which the authors read as suggesting mineralocorticoid excess during the disease. A significant rise in the fractional excretion of these electrolytes was seen in azotaemic infected dogs, a change the authors considered probably the result of acute tubular necrosis. Overall, the work relates altered renal handling of sodium and potassium to possible hormonal and tubular mechanisms in canine babesiosis.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** associational
- **Authors' conclusion:** A higher SUSPPUP ratio and potassium fractional excretion in B. canis-infected dogs suggested mineralocorticoid excess, and higher monovalent electrolyte excretion in azotaemic infected dogs probably resulted from acute tubular necrosis.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The findings show that urinary electrolyte handling differed between infected and healthy dogs and between azotaemic and non-azotaemic infected dogs. They are consistent with, but do not establish, aldosterone excess or tubular injury, because both are inferred from indices rather than measured directly. The report is a brief single-centre comparison.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** small sample; two-page short report; mineralocorticoid excess and acute tubular necrosis inferred, not directly measured; cross-sectional comparison without outcome data; recruitment period not stated; species method for PCR referred to an earlier paper.

_Abstract-level appraisal; not a formal risk-of-bias assessment._

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - Seventeen serum and urine sample sets came from dogs with babesiosis admitted to the Multiwet Small Animal Health Clinic (group A); twelve clinically healthy dogs formed the control group (group B) (full text).
  - B. canis infection was first diagnosed on blood smear and confirmed by a PCR method described in the authors' earlier paper; disease duration before admission was 1 to 7 days (full text).
  - Infected dogs were split by serum urea and creatinine into azotaemic (A1, 9 dogs) and non-azotaemic (A2, 8 dogs) groups; groups were compared with the Mann-Whitney U test (full text).
  - Median SUSPPUP ratio was 11.21 in infected dogs versus 3.37 in controls (p = 0.017836); median fractional excretion of potassium was 36.89% versus 4.455% (p = 0.000009) (full text).
  - Within infected dogs, median fractional excretion was higher in azotaemic than non-azotaemic dogs for sodium (1.35% vs 0.19%), potassium (66.13% vs 9.61%) and chloride (4.85% vs 0.39%) (full text).
  - Disease duration before treatment correlated positively with fractional excretion of potassium (r = 0.63), sodium (r = 0.89) and chloride (r = 0.79) (full text).
  - The authors proposed that renal azotaemia may be preceded by pre-renal azotaemia, that renal tubular disease contributes to hyponatraemia in azotaemic infected dogs, and that secondary Addison's disease could be excluded as its cause (full text).
