## Abstract-derived summary

This narrative review covers pathology and host immune responses in seven canine vector-borne diseases, among them babesiosis, ehrlichiosis, hepatozoonosis, anaplasmosis, bartonellosis, borreliosis and leishmaniosis. It stresses the three-way link joining the infectious agent, its arthropod vector and the canine immune system. It holds that diagnosis, treatment and prevention depend on a firm grasp of the underlying immunopathology. Knowledge of pathology and immunology, the author says, has lagged behind advances in molecular speciation and epidemiology. The review then describes commercially available vaccines for leishmaniosis, babesiosis and borreliosis, and frames immunological investigation as the basis for developing effective vaccines. Babesiosis is one of the seven diseases covered.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** review
- **Authors' conclusion:** Understanding of the pathology and immunology of canine vector-borne diseases lags behind advances in molecular characterisation, diagnosis and epidemiology, and the tools to close that gap are now generally available; new vaccines and immunotherapeutics will require applying them (full text).
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The article is a broad overview, and the Babesia-specific content is a small part of it: a few paragraphs on antibody-associated haemolytic anaemia and thrombocytopenia, and a vaccine section. It is useful for the immunopathology category as a statement of how little was known in 2011 about the canine immune response to Babesia. It reports no new data, so its statements are only as strong as the studies it cites.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** narrative review with no stated search or selection method; babesiosis is a minor share of a multi-disease text; most immunology discussed concerns leishmaniosis; species identification in the cited Babesia studies is not described; cited numbers are secondhand; no formal appraisal of the cited studies.

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - The paper lists the Babesia species of concern and their vectors in Table 1: B. vogeli, B. canis, B. rossi, other large Babesia, B. gibsoni, B. conradae and the B. microti-like agent (Theileria annae) (full text)
  - For haemolytic anaemia in canine babesiosis, B. gibsoni-infected dogs in Japan had IgG and IgM antibodies associated with red cells, directed at erythrocyte membrane antigens (full text)
  - A recent European study is cited as suggesting that Coombs test positivity is more likely with B. vogeli than with B. canis; an earlier North American study is cited as reporting Coombs positivity in 25 of 28 B. gibsoni cases and 6 of 9 B. canis cases (full text)
  - The review states that the thrombocytopenia of canine babesiosis may in part be immune mediated, with platelet-bound antibodies shown in B. gibsoni-infected dogs (full text)
  - The review notes that haemoplasma infections do not appear to trigger immune-mediated haemolytic anaemia in dogs, in contrast to the babesiosis findings (full text)
  - Vaccine section: two European babesiosis vaccines are described, one with soluble parasite antigens of B. canis and a bivalent one with antigens of B. canis and B. rossi (the text writes "Borrelia rossi", an apparent slip for Babesia rossi) (full text)
  - Beagles challenged with B. rossi after the bivalent vaccine are reported to have had reduced clinical signs, parasitaemia and plasma antigen concentrations; with B. canis challenge, clinical signs and plasma antigen were reduced but parasitaemia was not (full text)
  - The review suggests that vaccine-induced antibodies might prevent an initial vasodilatory shock leading to microvascular agglutination and hypercoagulation, a pathogenesis it describes as proposed for B. canis but not B. rossi infection (full text)
  - The author states that field efficacy data for the babesiosis vaccines are few and that tick control matters more than vaccination (full text)
  - The review states that, outside leishmaniosis, the host immune response to the other major canine vector-borne pathogens is poorly characterised; most work has addressed antibody (serological) responses, with very limited study of other aspects such as T-cell and cytokine responses (full text)
