## Abstract-derived summary

This review by Beugnet and Moreau describes babesiosis, a tick-borne disease in which Babesia protozoa invade the red blood cells of mammals. The authors set these parasites apart from Theileria, which can also enter white blood cells and endothelial cells, and note that diagnosis and treatment remain difficult for practising veterinarians. They link the growing importance of the disease to expanding tick habitats and greater animal movement, which carry parasites into new regions. Humans are occasionally infected, mainly people without a spleen or with weakened immunity, usually by a rodent parasite and sometimes by species from cattle or deer. Because recent advances in taxonomy, epidemiology, pathogenesis and control mostly concern the dog, whereas horse and cattle disease is still described in the classical way, the article concentrates on canine infection.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** review
- **Authors' conclusion:** Babesia infections remain common in domestic animals, and vaccine research has not yet produced an easy solution. The authors attribute the epidemiological changes mainly to shifts in tick distribution and activity, and name tick control as the route to prevention (full text).
- **Reviewer interpretation:** A broad narrative review of canine babesiosis whose pathophysiology content is a short section on pathogenesis and clinical signs. It describes haemolytic anaemia, the proposed immune-mediated component, and complicated disease linked to systemic inflammation and multiple organ dysfunction. Most of the paper covers taxonomy, epidemiology, diagnosis, treatment and prevention, so it is a secondary source for the syndromes rather than primary host-response data.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** narrative review with no stated search or selection methods; pathogenesis covered briefly; no original data or sample sizes; authors are affiliated with Merial and the text names Merial products (full text); scope extends beyond dogs to other hosts; species identity not tied to specific host data.
