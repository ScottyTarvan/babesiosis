## Abstract-derived summary

This review addresses why dogs infected with Babesia canis often show severe disease although few parasites are visible in circulating blood. It reports that large numbers of infected erythrocytes accumulate in the microvasculature, and proposes that sequestration starts when parasite molecules on the red cell surface bind ligands on capillary endothelium. Since parasites developing inside sequestered cells would be expected to rupture them, the persistence of infected cells at the attachment site needs further explanation. The author suggests that the coagulation system contributes by trapping both infected and uninfected erythrocytes once an infected cell has attached, and that newly released parasites then invade the trapped normal cells, eventually filling capillaries with parasitised erythrocytes.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** review
- **Authors' conclusion:** Microvascular sequestration, consolidated by coagulation-mediated trapping and local parasite proliferation, may explain persistent infection and severe signs despite low peripheral parasitaemia.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** Hypothesis-driven synthesis linking endothelial adhesion, immunothrombosis, hypotension and acute phase response in B. canis; useful for mechanism framing but several steps (local proliferation in clots, adhesion ligands) are proposed rather than demonstrated. It does not address other Babesia species or field-case heterogeneity.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** narrative review with no described search or selection method; evidence drawn mainly from one author's standardised experimental model (single isolate), limiting generalisability to natural infections and other species; hypotheses presented as such.

_Abstract-level appraisal; not a formal risk-of-bias assessment._
