## Abstract-derived summary
Cerebral malaria in African children involves small-vessel clotting and loss of blood-brain barrier integrity, but how Plasmodium falciparum produces this is unclear. The authors found raised circulating extracellular histones in patients, derived about equally from host and parasite, with higher values accompanying brain swelling on MRI. In postmortem brain tissue, histones lay alongside parasitised red cells lodged in small vessels, suggesting local release when schizonts burst, and histone staining on the vessel lining overlapped with thrombosis and leakage. Patient serum and purified parasite histones impaired barrier function and were toxic to cultured human brain endothelial cells, effects prevented by anti-histone antibody and non-anticoagulant heparin. The authors propose histone neutralisation merits testing.

## Critical interpretation
- **Evidence type:** observational human data (serum, MRI, postmortem tissue) combined with in-vitro mechanistic assays
- **Authors' conclusion:** Host and parasite histones contribute to thrombosis, barrier breakdown and brain swelling in cerebral malaria; neutralising agents such as non-anticoagulant heparin deserve exploration.
- **Relevance to canine babesiosis:** Shared pathways are brain microvascular sequestration and endothelial injury with microthrombosis. Canine studies measured sequestration-related or congestive change indirectly (CB041 hypothesis, CB080 necropsy, CB116 brain histology, CB069 clinical 'cerebral babesiosis') and systemic endothelial or thrombotic markers (CB004, CB145, CB011); none assessed barrier permeability or histones. Both connections are rated mechanistically plausible. Key differences: P. falciparum cytoadherence (PfEMP1) and schizont rupture have no demonstrated Babesia counterpart, the cohort is paediatric African, and heparin findings cannot be transferred to dogs.
- **Key limitations:** Abstract omits sample size; association with swelling is not causal; postmortem and in-vitro findings may not reflect living disease; no Babesia data.
