## Abstract-derived summary

This narrative review surveys recent literature on canine babesiosis, which the author presents as an emerging, clinically important vector-borne infection due to protozoan blood parasites. It covers how the Babesia and Theileria species found in dogs are classified, and where each is distributed around the world. Diagnosis is discussed across microscopy, molecular tests and serology. The review also summarises newer ideas about the pathophysiology of piroplasmosis, followed by current approaches to treating and preventing the disease. Pathophysiology is therefore one theme among several rather than the sole subject, and the abstract reports no data of its own, such as sample sizes or effect estimates, because the paper synthesises previously published work.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** review
- **Authors' conclusion:** Canine babesiosis remains a significant disease that poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges despite recent advances in understanding the pathogens and their pathophysiology. New piroplasm species are expected, and the ranges of established species are likely to expand (full text, Conclusion section).
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The review gives a broad orientation to canine piroplasm taxonomy, diagnosis and control, with a short pathophysiology section that links species to severity (full text). It is useful for context on species-level differences in virulence. Statements on severity and complications are drawn from cited studies and describe associations, not tested mechanisms.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** narrative review with no stated search method; pathophysiology is a small part of the content; no primary data; the cited studies vary in design and are not appraised; written in 2009, so later work is not covered.

  - B. gibsoni cases outside Asia are reported as arising from dog-to-dog transmission through biting and fighting, mainly in American Pit Bull Terrier-type dogs (full text)
  - B. vogeli is described as the least pathogenic of the well-recognised species in adult dogs and B. rossi as the most virulent (full text)
  - In B. rossi infection, haemoconcentration, neurological signs, acute renal failure and pulmonary oedema are described as complications that carry a poor prognosis (full text)
  - Haemolysis is described as intravascular and extravascular, involving parasite damage, osmotic fragility, oxidative injury and immune-mediated injury of the erythrocyte membrane (full text)
  - Cited Spanish study of Theileria annae: 36% of 58 dogs were azotaemic at diagnosis and had a 10-fold higher risk of death (full text)
  - Cited B. rossi study: urea and creatinine were unreliable indicators of renal damage, and mortality was associated with high cortisol and ACTH and low T4 and fT4 (full text)
  - Cited Italian PCR-RFLP study of 164 dogs: B. canis in 34 and B. vogeli in 11 (full text)
  - The text notes that PCR can give false-negative results in chronic infection, and that serology cannot separate acute from chronic infection (full text)
