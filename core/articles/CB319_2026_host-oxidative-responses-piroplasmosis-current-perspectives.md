## Abstract-derived summary

This critical narrative review examines how oxidative processes operate during piroplasm infection in dogs, cattle, buffaloes, small ruminants and horses, with cats discussed only to illustrate the limits of extrapolating between hosts. Sources up to 1 August 2026 were gathered by web-based searching, bibliographic records and citation chasing, and clinical, cellular, biochemical and pilot-treatment studies were kept separate. Infection commonly accompanies changed lipid peroxidation or antioxidant status, yet the direction of enzyme-level change depends on parasite species, severity, sample type and assay. Experimental canine work supports oxidative damage and accelerated clearance of parasitised and unparasitised red cells, whereas in transforming bovine theileriosis oxidants act as signals within infected leukocytes. Canine acetylcysteine trials and recent equine data do not establish prognostic cut-offs, organ protection or survival benefit. The authors propose a compartment-, stage- and time-resolved framework and call for harmonised sampling, longitudinal cohorts and controlled adjunct trials.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** review
- **Authors' conclusion:** The authors conclude that oxidative responses matter to piroplasmosis pathobiology but that their clinical value depends on identifying which process is measured, so a higher oxidant signal does not automatically justify antioxidant treatment.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** Relevant to host-parasite interaction and the oxidative contribution to erythrocyte injury in canine babesiosis, and a useful caution against inferring benefit from antioxidant therapy. Canine-specific findings are only a part of a multi-host synthesis, and the abstract gives no per-study numbers; it does not show that oxidative damage drives organ failure or outcome.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** abstract only (full text blocked); non-systematic, web-mediated search; multi-host scope dilutes canine specificity; the authors themselves cite inconsistent case definitions, small comparison groups, non-specific assays, incomplete control of haemolysis and single time-point sampling; peer review unverified, as the publisher has been flagged in predatory-publisher watchlists (reviewer background knowledge, not verified in this session) and no article-level peer-review information was obtained.
