## Abstract-derived summary

Two dogs were inoculated experimentally with Babesia microti to test whether the parasite can replicate in the canine host, and parasitaemia was followed over time. Red blood cell counts fell transiently after inoculation, and neither dog showed clinical signs during the observation period. Parasites were first seen inside red blood cells about five weeks after inoculation, and parasitaemia reached roughly one per cent by around day 50. Blood smears reacted with serum raised against B. microti, and PCR amplified parasite DNA whose sequence was 99 per cent identical to the 16S-like small subunit ribosomal RNA gene of B. microti. The authors concluded that B. microti is able to multiply in dogs and might become a source of canine babesiosis if ticks can transmit it between dogs.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** mechanistic (experimental)
- **Authors' conclusion:** B. microti replicates in dogs and could be a source of canine babesiosis if ticks can transmit it.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** In two experimentally inoculated dogs, low-level B. microti parasitaemia was accompanied by a transient fall in red cell counts without clinical signs. This is host-side erythrocyte evidence for a parasite that is usually described in rodents and humans. With two animals and no controls, the red cell change cannot be separated from sampling or other effects, and the findings are not evidence of clinical disease.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** two dogs only; no uninfected control group reported; no clinical disease, so little bearing on severity; red cell data given only qualitatively in the abstract; experimental inoculation may not reflect natural infection; abstract-level information only.
