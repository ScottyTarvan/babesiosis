## Abstract-derived summary

This review covers canine babesiosis, a protozoal tick-borne disease with a worldwide distribution. It notes that vector-borne infections are a major health concern for dogs globally, and that recent reports describe rising case numbers across Europe, Poland included. The authors state that molecular methods have increased knowledge of canine babesiosis, its prevalence, and its clinical and pathological features. They argue that accurate identification of the species responsible is needed to improve diagnostic and therapeutic methods and to predict the course of disease. The review gathers recent knowledge on Babesia species and on B. canis. The abstract does not report original data, sample sizes or outcome measures.

## Critical interpretation

- **Evidence type:** review
- **Authors' conclusion:** The abstract states no conclusion; the stated aim is to summarise recent knowledge on Babesia species and B. canis, supporting accurate species identification for diagnosis, treatment and prediction of the disease course.
- **Reviewer interpretation:** The review bears on host-side pathophysiology mainly through its section on host immune response, which draws largely on non-canine models, and through brief clinical and haematological descriptions. Much of the remainder covers parasite genomics, diagnostics and vaccines. It is useful as background on immunity and strain diversity, but it offers little canine pathophysiological data.
- **Key limitations (from available information):** narrative review with no stated search or selection method; no original data; immunity evidence drawn mostly from rodent, bovine and equine hosts; genomic content mostly non-canine; large parts concern parasite biology and diagnostics rather than host pathophysiology.

- **Information beyond the abstract:**
  - The immunity section describes IgM, IgG1 and IgG2 as the antibody isotypes specific for Babesia infection and links antibodies to opsonisation of parasitised red cells; the supporting experiments cited were in equines and calves (full text)
  - Cell-mediated and innate immunity (spleen, T cells, NK cells, macrophages, IFN-gamma, TNF-alpha, nitric oxide) are described mainly from B. microti rodent work and a theoretical model with establishment, progression and resolution stages (full text)
  - An experimental B. canis infection cited in the review is described as producing transient low parasitaemia (1%-2%), lower haematocrit, thrombocytopenia, prolonged APTT and hypotension (full text)
  - Clinical suspicion signs listed for canine babesiosis include lethargy, anorexia, fever, icterus, abnormal urine, renal failure, hypotension, acute respiratory distress syndrome, enlarged spleen and tachycardia; blood findings listed are thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, lymphopenia, neutropenia and low red cell count, packed cell volume and haemoglobin (full text)
  - Dogs vaccinated with soluble parasite antigens of one B. canis strain were reported as protected against homologous but not heterologous strain challenge; antigenic variation is stated not to have been shown for B. canis (full text)
  - The review reports genetic diversity among B. canis isolates (Bc28 groups) and cites an association between strain and virulence in Polish strains (full text)
  - The two commercial canine vaccines available in Europe are described as altering the course of disease without preventing infection (full text)
  - Genomic tables cover other apicomplexans and bovine or human Babesia species; no canine Babesia genome is tabulated (full text)
